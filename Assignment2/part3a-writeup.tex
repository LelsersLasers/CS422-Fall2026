\section*{Summary}

We repeated the Question 1 and 2 measurements with CUBIC, Reno, and BBR on
five public iperf3 servers, three times each (44 of 45 tests completed).
\textbf{BBR had the highest goodput on every destination in every
repetition} (78.1 Mbit/s mean vs.\ 52.1 for CUBIC and 43.6 for Reno), but it
had 12--19\,ms more queueing delay than CUBIC and Reno on every path and
retransmitted about 11\% of its bytes. CUBIC beat Reno in 13 of 14 matched comparisons, and the gap grew with
path RTT. Our paths were in a \emph{loss-limited} regime: CUBIC and Reno kept
a roughly constant window of 530--610 segments on paths from 48 to 130\,ms, so their
goodput fell roughly as $1/\text{RTT}$, while BBR, which does not treat loss
as a congestion signal, was not limited this way.

\section*{Metrics}

All metrics come from \texttt{TCP\_INFO} on the data socket, as in Question 2.
Per test we compute
(\href{\repo/part3/run_algorithms.py\#L126-L158}{\texttt{path\_metrics}}):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Goodput:} $8\,\Delta\texttt{tcpi\_bytes\_acked}/\Delta t$ per
  interval, summarized by min, median, mean, and p95.
\item \textbf{RTT:} the kernel's smoothed RTT (\texttt{tcpi\_rtt}); mean and
  p95 over the test.
\item \textbf{min RTT:} \texttt{tcpi\_min\_rtt} at the end of the test, our
  estimate of the path's propagation delay.
\item \textbf{Queueing delay:} mean RTT $-$ min RTT.
\item \textbf{Loss proxy:} retransmitted bytes as a percentage of bytes sent
  (\texttt{tcpi\_bytes\_retrans}/\texttt{tcpi\_bytes\_sent}), plus the
  per-interval retransmission count \texttt{delta\_retrans} used in the plots.
\item \textbf{BDP estimate:} median goodput $\times$ min RTT. This uses the
  goodput the algorithm \emph{achieved}, so it is a lower bound on the path's
  true bandwidth-delay product, not a capacity measurement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Analysis}

\subsection*{What the traces show}

Figure~\ref{fig:ts} shows each algorithm's characteristic window behavior.
\textbf{Reno} grows its window linearly, by about one segment per RTT: from
roughly 450 to 700 segments over 40\,s at a 130\,ms RTT, before a single
loss halves it. \textbf{CUBIC} shows its cubic sawtooth: after each loss it
drops to 70\% of the window, grows back quickly, flattens out near the
previous maximum, and then probes past it. It hits a loss every few seconds
to about 15\,s.
\textbf{BBR}'s window is much larger (1,300--3,300 segments) and oscillates
with its ProbeBW pacing cycle. It collapses almost to zero at about 12, 31,
and 48\,s; these are BBR's ProbeRTT phases, in which it drains the queue to
re-measure the minimum RTT. Reno's large retransmission spike at about 2\,s
is the slow-start overshoot, which also accounts for most of Reno's
retransmitted bytes on this path.

In the scatter plots (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}), CUBIC and Reno samples
cluster at windows of 300--800 segments and goodput of 25--75\,Mbit/s, with
almost no retransmissions. BBR's samples sit at larger windows and higher
goodput, and BBR is the only algorithm with sustained retransmissions:
hundreds of retransmitted segments per interval while still delivering
40--125\,Mbit/s.

\subsection*{The operating regime: loss-limited, not capacity-limited}

The most important pattern is in Table~\ref{tab:dest}. CUBIC and Reno kept
nearly the same mean window, 530--610 segments (about 0.8\,MB), on four
paths whose minimum RTTs range from 48 to 130\,ms. Since sending rate is
roughly $\text{cwnd}/\text{RTT}$, their goodput fell roughly as
$1/\text{RTT}$, for example from 85.7\,Mbit/s at 48\,ms to 36.0\,Mbit/s at
130\,ms for Reno. If these flows were limited by bottleneck capacity, the
window would instead grow to match each path's BDP.

A roughly constant window is what the classic AIMD throughput model predicts
when the window is limited by the loss rate $p$ rather than by capacity:
\[
\text{throughput} \approx \frac{\text{MSS}}{\text{RTT}}\cdot\frac{1.22}{\sqrt{p}},
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \text{cwnd} \approx \frac{1.22}{\sqrt{p}}.
\]
Our Reno trace is consistent with this. One loss in about 40\,s at about
45\,Mbit/s is one loss per roughly 155,000 segments ($p\approx 6\times
10^{-6}$), which gives $\text{cwnd}\approx 1.22/\sqrt{p}\approx 480$
segments, close to the 530--565 we measured. The losses are sparse: Reno and
CUBIC retransmitted under about 1\% of bytes, and most of that came from
slow start. Each such loss still makes them cut the window, so they rarely
grow large enough to fill the path.

BBR is not limited this way, because it does not use loss as its congestion
signal. It estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT and paces at
that rate, keeping up to about $2\times$ the estimated BDP in flight (its
window gain). This is why it reached 1.5--2.1$\times$ Reno's goodput on every
path. It also shows that the paths had more capacity than CUBIC and Reno were
using.

\subsection*{When each algorithm performs better}

\textbf{Throughput: BBR, increasingly so on long paths.} BBR had the highest
goodput on every destination in every repetition (15 of 15 comparisons). Its
advantage over Reno grew with RTT, from $1.5$--$1.8\times$ on the
48--130\,ms paths to $2.1\times$ on the 288\,ms path. With a sparse loss
rate, a loss-based algorithm needs a window close to the BDP to fill the pipe,
and after each backoff it needs more round trips to regrow on a long path,
whereas BBR's rate does not depend on loss. BBR is the better choice for bulk
transfer on high-BDP paths with sparse non-congestive loss, the situation it
was designed for.

\textbf{CUBIC vs.\ Reno: CUBIC on high-BDP paths, about equal on short
ones.} CUBIC beat Reno in 13 of 14 matched comparisons. The gap was small on
short paths ($1.05$--$1.09\times$ at 48--109\,ms), $1.15\times$ at 130\,ms,
and large on the longest one
($1.48\times$ at 288\,ms, where CUBIC's mean window was 834 segments vs.\
565 for Reno). Two design differences explain this. CUBIC backs off to 70\% of
its window instead of 50\%, so it loses less after each loss. More
importantly, its window grows as a function of \emph{time since the last
loss}, $W(t)=C(t-K)^3+W_{\max}$, independent of RTT, whereas Reno adds one
segment \emph{per RTT}. On a 288\,ms path, Reno needs over 2 minutes to
regrow 500 segments, while CUBIC regrows in seconds. On short-RTT,
small-BDP paths, Reno's per-RTT growth is fast enough that the two behave
similarly.

\textbf{Delay: Reno and CUBIC.} Reno and CUBIC had similar queueing delay
(23.8 and 25.7\,ms mean; each was lower on some destinations), and BBR had
the highest on every destination (38.6\,ms mean). Part of this delay is common to all paths and algorithms:
the 22--28\,ms floor appears even for Reno on the shortest path, so it is
probably jitter from our own access link (Wi-Fi) and the VM's virtual
network rather than queueing on the Internet path. Above that floor, BBR added
a consistent 12--19\,ms of standing queue compared with the lower of CUBIC
and Reno on the same path. BBRv1 keeps up to $2\times$ the
estimated BDP in flight and periodically paces at 1.25$\times$ its estimated
bandwidth to probe for more, and that extra data waits in the bottleneck
buffer. CUBIC and Reno back off as soon as the buffer overflows, so they hold
less standing queue. For latency-sensitive traffic on these paths, CUBIC or
Reno is preferable.

\textbf{Loss: BBR causes it, CUBIC avoids it.} BBR retransmitted 7--16\% of
its bytes on every destination, compared with under about 1\% for CUBIC and
Reno. Because BBR does not slow down when packets are dropped, its probing
overflows the bottleneck buffer repeatedly, and the dropped packets are
resent. Its \emph{goodput} is still highest because the retransmissions are
excluded and it keeps the link busy, but this wastes capacity, and a BBR flow
sharing a buffer with CUBIC or Reno flows would cause them losses and back
them off. We did not measure competing flows, so this fairness cost is a
prediction, not an observation.

\textbf{Relating to the BDP.} The achieved-goodput BDP lower bound grows from
about 0.5--0.9\,MB on the shorter paths to 0.8--1.5\,MB on the 288\,ms path
(\texttt{bdp\_kbytes} in \texttt{comparison\_summary.csv}). CUBIC and Reno's
mean windows of about 0.8\,MB are near the BDP on the short paths but far
below it on the long one, which is where they fall furthest behind BBR. BBR's
windows of 2,000--3,200 segments (2.8--4.6\,MB) are well above the BDP; the
excess is what shows up as queueing delay and retransmissions.

\textbf{Regimes we did not observe.} With a deep, bloated buffer and no
random loss, CUBIC and Reno would fill the buffer and BBR would be expected to
have the \emph{lower} RTT. On a short, uncongested path all three should
reach link capacity and behave similarly. None of our five paths was in either
regime, so we cannot confirm these predictions from our data.

\subsection*{Variability across runs}

On a single destination, results were consistent across repetitions: the
coefficient of variation of per-run mean goodput was 1.5--9.4\% for 13 of the
15 destination--algorithm pairs (Table~\ref{tab:dest}, Figure~\ref{fig:var}).
The exceptions were CUBIC (18.3\%) and Reno (12.2\%) on the long Mauritius
path, where a single loss costs the most and CUBIC's first run was much lower
(23.7 vs.\ about 33\,Mbit/s). By contrast, goodput across \emph{different}
destinations varied by a factor of 3--4 for every algorithm. The path
matters more than the repetition, which is why we compare algorithms on the
same path within minutes of each other.

The ranking was stable across runs and test orders: BBR was fastest in all
15 destination--repetition groups, and CUBIC beat Reno in 13 of 14 (the
exception, MilkyWan in repetition 2, was a 0.9\,Mbit/s difference). Because
the order rotated, each algorithm ran first, second, and third once on every
path, so a ranking caused by test order would have changed across
repetitions; it did not.

\subsection*{Limitations of comparisons over the public Internet}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Paths change.} Cross traffic, routing, and server load change
  between tests. Back-to-back testing and rotation reduce but do not remove
  this effect, and three repetitions per pair is a small sample.
\item \textbf{One flow at a time.} We never ran algorithms against each
  other, so we cannot measure fairness or how BBR's losses would affect
  competing flows.
\item \textbf{Unknown bottleneck.} We cannot see where the bottleneck is or
  how large its buffer is. Our host was a VM behind macOS NAT on Wi-Fi, which
  adds delay jitter and may be the bottleneck on some paths. The common
  22--28\,ms queueing-delay floor suggests this.
\item \textbf{Servers.} Public iperf3 servers can rate-limit or refuse tests
  (one of our tests was refused three times), and their receive windows and
  load are unknown.
\item \textbf{BDP is a lower bound.} We do not know the true bottleneck
  bandwidth, so our BDP estimate uses achieved goodput.
\item \textbf{Measurement granularity.} TCP\_INFO is sampled every 0.2\,s and
  RTT is the kernel's smoothed estimate, so short bursts of loss or delay are
  averaged, and individual duplicate-ACK and timeout events are not
  separated.
\item \textbf{One BBR version.} Linux 6.8 ships BBRv1; BBRv2/v3 react to loss
  and would likely retransmit much less.
\end{itemize}
